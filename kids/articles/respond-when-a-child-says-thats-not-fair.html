What to Say When a Child Says “That’s Not Fair”

Respond to “That’s not fair” with a calm five-step conversation that separates equal from fair, validates disappointment, and finds the next workable action.

By Morteza Moezodini and Zohre Rahimi · For parents, teachers, and caregivers of ages 4–8

“That’s not fair” can mean many things: “I wanted the same amount,” “I did not understand the rule,” “Someone got an advantage,” or simply “I hate this outcome.” The phrase is a starting point for investigation, not proof that the child is selfish or correct.

Quick answer

Pause, identify what “fair” means in this moment, state the rule or need, offer the next choice, and revisit only if new information appears.

Why this skill can be difficult

Children ages 4–8 are building ideas about equality, rules, need, effort, and turn order. Adults can validate the disappointment without agreeing with every claim, then make the situation concrete enough to solve.

Ages 4–8 cover a wide range of language, memory, self-control, and social experience. A child may understand the idea in a calm conversation and still need an adult beside them when the real moment arrives. Teach the sequence before the difficult situation, reduce the number of words during stress, and return to reflection only when the child can participate.

A practical six-step routine

  1. Reflect the disappointment. Begin with the result the child wanted instead of arguing about the word fair.
  2. Ask one clarifying question. Find out whether the concern is amount, turn order, rule changes, special help, or an unexpected outcome.
  3. Name the kind of fairness. Explain whether the situation uses equal shares, equal turns, different support, safety, or a prior agreement.
  4. Check the rule. If the adult or child changed the rule midway, acknowledge it and repair the process.
  5. Offer the next action. Choose a wait, trade, appeal, reset, or acceptance step that can happen now.
  6. End repeated debate kindly. Once the explanation and choice are clear, hold the boundary without adding a longer courtroom argument.

Words an adult can use

Short, respectful sentences are easier to remember than a lecture. Use language that describes the situation, protects safety, and gives the child a next action.

  • “You wanted the same turn, and the order surprised you.”
  • “Do you mean the amounts are different, or the rule changed?”
  • “Fair does not always mean identical. Sam has extra help so both of you can take part.”
  • “You may disagree. The next choice is wait here or choose another activity.”

A playful activity to try

Use twelve blocks and three toy characters. Create different situations: equal shares, one character needs a ramp, one arrived later, and one rule changes unexpectedly. Ask the child to decide what fairness means in each scene and explain one possible repair.

Keep the first practice short enough to end successfully. Switch roles when possible so the child experiences both sides of the interaction. Later, refer back to the game with one familiar cue instead of teaching the whole lesson again.

How to use the idea at home

State family rules before high-interest situations, especially desserts, devices, and turns. When exceptions are necessary, give a short reason without sharing another child’s private information.

How to use the idea in a classroom

Teachers can distinguish equal materials, equitable support, and rotating opportunities. A visible turn system makes fairness less dependent on who argues most loudly.

Adjust the support by age

Ages 4–5

Use concrete objects and short language: same, turn, need, safe, and promised. Too many explanations can sound like negotiation.

Ages 6–8

Invite children to compare different fair solutions and identify which rule was agreed before the activity. They can also learn a respectful appeal process.

A common mistake to avoid

Avoid saying “Life is not fair” as the entire response. It may be true in a broad sense, but it does not help the child understand this rule, detect a real injustice, or choose a next action.

Progress may be the child clarifying the complaint, accepting an explanation sooner, or using an appeal without yelling. Fairness questions are part of moral development and will not disappear.

Quick checklist

  • Reflect the disappointment is clear enough to use without a long explanation.
  • Ask one clarifying question is clear enough to use without a long explanation.
  • Name the kind of fairness is clear enough to use without a long explanation.
  • Check the rule is clear enough to use without a long explanation.
  • Offer the next action is clear enough to use without a long explanation.
  • The child has a safe way to pause, ask for help, or try again.
  • The adult reviews progress without shame or comparison.

Frequently asked questions

Should I always explain why?

Give a brief reason when possible. You do not need to reveal private details or debate the same answer indefinitely.

What if the child is right?

Acknowledge the mistake, repair what can be repaired, and explain how the rule will be clearer next time.

How is fair different from equal?

Equal means the same amount or treatment. Fair may include different support so people can participate safely and meaningfully.

What if siblings compare everything?

Use clear family systems for turns and privileges, while avoiding constant scorekeeping across different ages and needs.

Can children challenge adult decisions?

Yes, through respectful questions or a defined appeal time. Safety limits may still require immediate compliance.

Final takeaway

Pause, identify what “fair” means in this moment, state the rule or need, offer the next choice, and revisit only if new information appears. Begin with one small practice and repeat it in a familiar setting. The purpose is not perfect behavior or instant independence; it is a child who has clearer language, safer options, and a reliable adult relationship while the skill develops.
