# The Next-Person Test: How to Review AI-Assisted Work Before You Send It

AI can make a document look finished before the thinking is finished.

That is useful when the tool helps you organize notes, compare alternatives, draft a first version, or make a messy idea clearer. It becomes a problem when the polished output creates hidden work for the next person.

A concise summary that saves the recipient time is leverage.

A polished summary that forces the recipient to reopen the source material, check every claim, recover missing context, and make the decision you were supposed to make is not the same thing.

That distinction is at the center of *The Last Human Pass*: after AI has helped, a human still has to decide whether the work is actually ready to leave their hands.

One practical way to do that is the **Next-Person Test**.

## What is the Next-Person Test?

The Next-Person Test asks a simple question:

**Will this AI-assisted work help the next person begin their part, or will it quietly make them finish mine?**

The idea is not that you should do everyone else’s job. Good handoffs still respect ownership. A manager may own the final decision. A client may own implementation. A hiring manager may own selection.

Your responsibility is different: remove avoidable confusion, cleanup, verification burden, and unfinished thinking that you could reasonably resolve before sending.

That is the difference between a task being complete on your screen and a handoff being useful in someone else’s hands.

## Five questions to ask before you send AI-assisted work

### 1. What does the recipient need to do next?

Start with the purpose of the handoff.

Are they supposed to:

- approve something,
- choose between options,
- review a recommendation,
- make a decision,
- edit language,
- verify data,
- take an action,
- simply stay informed?

If you cannot describe the next action in one sentence, the recipient may not be able to either.

AI often generates more material than the task actually needs. The first human responsibility is to make the purpose clear.

### 2. What questions will they immediately have?

Read the work from the recipient’s side.

What will make them stop?

Common examples:

- “Where did this number come from?”
- “Which option are you recommending?”
- “What am I supposed to review?”
- “Which assumptions are confirmed?”

If you can predict the question now, you may be able to answer it before sending.

That does not mean making the document longer. Often the best fix is one sentence, a source link, a clear label, or a direct recommendation.

### 3. What will they have to verify?

This question matters because fluent AI output can sound more certain than the underlying evidence deserves.

If a claim affects a decision, ask what verification belongs on your side of the handoff.

That may include:

- checking a number against the original spreadsheet,
- confirming a quotation against the source,
- making sure a policy is current,
- checking whether a recommendation fits the actual project constraints,
- verifying that a cited source supports the claim being made.

NIST’s Generative AI Profile treats generative-AI risk management as something organizations should govern, map, measure, and manage rather than treating generated output as automatically trustworthy. For everyday knowledge work, the practical lesson is straightforward: the level of human verification should rise with the consequence of being wrong.

A low-stakes rewrite and a board recommendation do not need the same review process.

### 4. What context have I assumed instead of explaining?

AI can produce a coherent document from incomplete context.

That can be dangerous precisely because the gaps may no longer look like gaps.

Before sending, ask whether the recipient knows:

- what constraints shaped the work,
- which data was included or excluded,
- what the team already decided,
- what assumptions were made,
- which version of a document was used,
- where uncertainty remains,
- what changed since the previous draft.

Context does not need to become an essay. But important context should not remain trapped in your head while the document speaks with artificial confidence.

### 5. What decision was I supposed to make that I quietly left for them?

This is often the most revealing question.

Imagine your manager asks:

> “Review the customer feedback and tell me whether we should delay the launch.”

AI can quickly produce:

- seven themes,
- a sentiment summary,
- a risk matrix,
- a table of complaints,
- a list of options,
- an executive summary.

That can look like substantial work.

But if you send all of it without answering whether you recommend delaying the launch, the hardest part of your responsibility has simply moved downstream.

You delivered information instead of judgment.

The recipient still has to complete the task you were assigned.

## A useful handoff is not the same as a large handoff

Generative AI makes volume cheap.

That changes the meaning of professional editing.

The question is no longer only, “Can I produce a comprehensive document?”

It is also:

- Does the recipient need all of this?
- What can be removed?
- What is the actual recommendation?
- Which uncertainty matters?
- What evidence deserves to stay visible?
- What decision or action should happen next?

A 20-page output is not automatically more useful than a two-page brief.

The best human pass often reduces output rather than adding to it.

## Use the test before the prompt, not only after the draft

The Next-Person Test is even stronger when it shapes the AI request itself.

Before generating, define:

**Recipient:** Who is this for?

**Action:** What do they need to do next?

**Decision:** What question should this work help answer?

**Evidence:** What must be verified or sourced?

**Constraints:** What context must the model not guess?

**Format:** What is the shortest useful form for the handoff?

That changes the prompt from “make this comprehensive” to something closer to “help me produce the smallest complete artifact the recipient can actually use.”

## A 60-second pre-send version

You do not need a formal checklist for every email.

For routine work, use this fast version:

1. **Purpose:** Is it clear what this is for?
2. **Truth:** Did I verify the consequential claims?
3. **Context:** Did I include what the recipient needs to interpret it?
4. **Judgment:** Did I make the decision or recommendation I actually own?
5. **Next step:** Can the recipient tell what happens now?

If the answer to all five is yes, the handoff is probably stronger.

If one answer is no, that is where the last human pass should focus.

## This is not an argument against AI at work

Good AI use can reduce total work by helping people summarize, compare alternatives, improve organization, challenge assumptions, and reduce repetitive drafting. The standard is whether that efficiency survives the handoff.

If you save time but the recipient has to reconstruct what you meant, the work did not disappear. It moved.

## FAQ

### Do I need to verify every sentence AI produces?

Not every sentence carries the same risk. Verification should match the stakes. Claims that affect decisions, money, safety, compliance, reputation, or external commitments deserve more scrutiny than low-stakes wording suggestions.

### Does the Next-Person Test mean I should make every document perfect?

No. The standard is not perfection. It is ownership. Finish the part you reasonably own, make uncertainty visible when it matters, and do not leave avoidable cleanup disguised as finished work.

### Should I always disclose that AI helped?

Disclosure depends on context, policy, expectations, and the nature of the task. The broader principle is that AI assistance should never become a way to avoid responsibility for the work carrying your name.

### What is the simplest sign that a handoff is weak?

If the recipient has to ask, “What do you want me to do with this?” the handoff probably needs another pass.

## The final question

Before you send, ask:

**Did AI reduce the total work required, or did it only move work from me to the next person?**

That question changes how you edit.

It changes what you verify.

It changes how much you send.

And it keeps the most important part of AI-assisted professional work where it belongs: with the human who is responsible for the handoff.
